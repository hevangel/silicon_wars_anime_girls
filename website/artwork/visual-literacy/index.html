The TSMC Visual Literacy Series

A three-part course · one character · hundreds of controlled comparisons

Learn to read pictures
the way you read sentences.

Every picture in this series shows the same person — TSMC, the Quiet Queenmaker of Silicon Wars — so that the only thing left to study is the picture-making itself. The series borrows its shape from linguistics, loosely and on purpose: Part I treats styles as languages and dialects — reusable libraries of marks and conventions. Part II learns the vocabulary and grammar: the individual visual decisions and how they combine. Part III turns to semantics and pragmatics: what pictures mean, and what they do to the people who see them.

The skill it teaches is the AI-era one: recognize a concept, name it accurately, specify an intention, generate an experiment, and judge whether the result actually communicates — then refine the instruction from what you observed. Drawing skill is welcome here, never required.

The Visual Language Stack

A pedagogical model — our own synthesis, inspired by established research in visual language, formal analysis and iconography (Kress & van Leeuwen's Reading Images, Neil Cohn's visual-language research, the Getty's formal-analysis guides, Iconclass). Not a strict hierarchy: real pictures use every layer at once. It is a map for finding which layer a problem lives in.

    A recommended path

    1. Start anywhere, finish in order. Each part stands alone, but the argument builds: languages (I) are made of decisions (II) that carry meanings (III).
    2. Do the looking exercises. Every lesson ends with one. Covering labels and guessing first is where the learning happens.
    3. Use the registry below. When a word surprises you — in any part — find it in the concept registry and follow it across the other two courses.
    4. End at Part III, Lesson 25. Intended vs perceived meaning is the exam the whole series sets: an image succeeds when its intended audience reads the intended meaning.

    Concept registry

    Concepts shared across the three courses, harvested from their own structured data: the styles that tend to use a decision, the cards that teach it, the groups that put it to work meaning something. Relationships are tendencies, not rules — “red means anger” is exactly the kind of shortcut Part III teaches you to distrust. Click a concept to see its trail.
